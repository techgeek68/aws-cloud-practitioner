# Chapter 6: The AWS Frameworks

AWS publishes two frameworks that appear on both exams and in real project work. They answer different questions and are often confused.

- **The AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (AWS CAF)** answers "how does an organization move to the cloud?" It is about people, governance, and sequencing, and its audience is largely the business.

- **The AWS Well-Architected Framework** answers "is this workload built well?" It is about technical design, and its audience is architects and engineers.

CAF is used before and during a transformation. Well-Architected is used continuously, for the life of every workload. Sections 6.1 to 6.3 cover CAF, and sections 6.4 to 6.7 cover Well-Architected.

---
## 6.1 The AWS Cloud Adoption Framework

The AWS CAF is a set of best practices, guidance, and tools for planning and running a cloud transformation. Its central claim is that cloud transformations rarely fail for technical reasons. They fail on skills, funding models, governance, and organizational change, so those are treated as first-class concerns rather than afterthoughts.

**What organizations use it for**

- Identify and prioritize transformation opportunities and tie them to business outcomes.

- Assess current cloud readiness and find the capability gaps that would block progress.

- Build a transformation roadmap and revise it as the program runs.


**Structure**

The current version is CAF 3.0, which is organized in three layers that work together rather than in sequence:

- **Six perspectives** identify who is responsible, covering 47 discrete capabilities in total, up from 31 in the previous version.

- **Four transformation domains** identify what is changing.

- **Four phases** identify where you are in the journey.

## 6.2 The Six CAF Perspectives

The first three perspectives are largely business-facing and the last three largely technical, though every one of them requires both sides in the room.

### 6.2.1 Business Perspective

- **Focus:** ensuring cloud investment accelerates digital transformation and delivers the intended business outcomes.

- **Capabilities:** strategy management, portfolio management, innovation management, product management, strategic partnership, data monetization, business insights, and data science.

- **Stakeholders:** CEO, CFO, COO, CIO, CTO, CMO, CPO.

### 6.2.2 People Perspective

- **Focus:** the bridge between technology and business, covering culture, workforce, and organizational change.

- **Capabilities:** culture evolution, transformational leadership, cloud fluency, workforce transformation, change acceleration, organization design, and organizational alignment.

- **Stakeholders:** CIO, COO, CTO, HR leaders, cloud directors.

### 6.2.3 Governance Perspective

- **Focus:** orchestrating cloud initiatives so value is maximized and risk is minimized.

- **Capabilities:** program and project management, benefits management, risk management, cloud financial management, application portfolio management, and data governance and curation.

- **Stakeholders:** CIO, CTO, CFO, Chief Data Officer, Chief Risk Officer.

### 6.2.4 Platform Perspective

- **Focus:** building the technical foundation on which workloads are built, modernized, and scaled.

- **Capabilities:** platform architecture, data architecture, platform engineering, data engineering, provisioning and orchestration, modern application development, and continuous integration and delivery.

- **Stakeholders:** CTO, IT managers, solutions architects, engineers.

### 6.2.5 Security Perspective

- **Focus:** achieving confidentiality, integrity, and availability objectives for data and workloads.

- **Capabilities:** security governance, security assurance, identity and access management, threat detection, vulnerability management, infrastructure protection, data protection, application security, and incident response.

- **Stakeholders:** CISO, Chief Compliance Officer, security architects, auditors.

### 6.2.6 Operations Perspective

- **Focus:** delivering and running cloud services day to day at the level the business requires.

- **Capabilities:** observability, event management, incident and problem management, change and release management, performance and capacity management, configuration management, patch management, availability and continuity management, and application management.

- **Stakeholders:** IT operations managers, support managers, site reliability engineers.

A memory aid for the exam: the three business-facing perspectives are Business, People, and Governance, and the three technical ones are Platform, Security, and Operations. Questions usually describe a symptom and ask which perspective owns it. Training and cultural resistance point to People. Compliance and portfolio oversight point to Governance. Monitoring and incident handling point to Operations.

## 6.3 Cloud Transformation Domains, Phases, and Outcomes

### 6.3.1 The Four Transformation Domains

| Domain | What changes |
| --- | --- |
| Technology | Migrating and modernizing legacy infrastructure, applications, and data platforms |
| Process | Digitizing, automating, and optimizing business operations |
| Organization | Reimagining the operating model and how teams are structured and work together |
| Product | Creating new value propositions, business models, and customer experiences |

### 6.3.2 The Four Phases

These are iterative and incremental rather than a one-time waterfall. Each new workload or business domain can be taken through them again.

1. **Envision.** Identify transformation opportunities and connect them to specific business outcomes. Usually run as a facilitated workshop.

2. **Align.** Identify capability gaps across the six perspectives, surface stakeholder concerns and dependencies, and produce an action plan.

3. **Launch.** Deliver pilots in production, prove incremental business value, and learn enough to adjust before committing further.

4. **Scale.** Expand what worked to the intended scale, and tie continued investment to demonstrated benefit.

The distinction most often tested is between Envision and Align. Envision is about identifying opportunity and outcome. Align is about identifying gaps and building the plan to close them.

### 6.3.3 Desired Outcomes

AWS states four outcomes that a well-run transformation should deliver:

- Reduced business risk.

- Improved environmental, social, and governance performance.

- Increased revenue growth.

- Improved operational efficiency.

Note the balance. Only one of the four is a cost or efficiency measure. Framing a cloud program purely as a cost-reduction exercise misses three quarters of the stated value and tends to produce migrations that save little and change nothing.

## 6.4 The AWS Well-Architected Framework

The Well-Architected Framework is a set of design principles and questions for evaluating cloud architectures. Its purpose is to make architectural trade offs explicit rather than accidental.

- It does not prescribe a single correct architecture. It surfaces the questions a good architecture should have answers to.

- It is organized into six pillars, each with its own design principles and best practices.

- It is applied through a structured review of a specific workload, not of an entire estate at once.

- Reviews are repeated over time, because a workload that was well architected two years ago may not be today.

There are also Well-Architected Lenses, which extend the framework with domain-specific guidance for areas such as serverless, machine learning, SaaS, data analytics, and financial services.

Two points worth internalizing early:

- **The pillars conflict with each other, deliberately.** Higher reliability usually costs more. Tighter security often costs performance. The framework exists to make those trade-offs conscious and documented, not to pretend they can all be maximized at once.

- **A trade-off is not a failure.** Choosing lower reliability for a batch job that can rerun is good architecture, provided the choice was deliberate and is written down.

## 6.5 The Six Pillars

This section is the course's definition of the pillars. Chapter 16 applies them to design decisions, and Part III returns to them repeatedly.

### 6.5.1 Operational Excellence

- **Definition:** running and monitoring systems to deliver business value, and continually improving processes and procedures.
- **Design principles:** perform operations as code; make frequent, small, reversible changes; refine operations procedures frequently; anticipate failure; learn from all operational failures.
- **AWS examples:** AWS CloudFormation for infrastructure as code, AWS Systems Manager for runbooks and automation, Amazon CloudWatch for operational visibility.

### 6.5.2 Security

- **Definition:** protecting data, systems, and assets, and using cloud technologies to improve security posture.

- **Design principles:** implement a strong identity foundation; enable traceability; apply security at all layers; automate security best practices; protect data in transit and at rest; keep people away from data; prepare for security events.

- **AWS examples:** AWS IAM for least privilege, AWS CloudTrail and AWS Config for traceability, AWS KMS for encryption, security groups and AWS WAF for layered protection.

### 6.5.3 Reliability

- **Definition:** the ability of a workload to perform its intended function correctly and consistently, and to recover from failure.

- **Design principles:** automatically recover from failure; test recovery procedures; scale horizontally to increase aggregate availability; stop guessing capacity; manage change through automation.

- **AWS examples:** Amazon RDS Multi-AZ for automatic failover, EC2 Auto Scaling for horizontal scale and replacement of unhealthy instances, Amazon Route 53 health checks for failover routing.

### 6.5.4 Performance Efficiency

- **Definition:** using computing resources efficiently to meet requirements, and maintaining that efficiency as demand and technology change.

- **Design principles:** democratize advanced technologies by consuming them as managed services; go global in minutes; use serverless architectures; experiment more often; consider mechanical sympathy, meaning choose the technology that matches how the workload actually behaves.

- **AWS examples:** AWS Lambda to avoid provisioning for idle capacity, Amazon CloudFront to reduce latency, Amazon ElastiCache to remove repeated database work, right sized instance families.

### 6.5.5 Cost Optimization

- **Definition:** running systems to deliver business value at the lowest price point.

- **Design principles:** implement cloud financial management; adopt a consumption model; measure overall efficiency; stop spending money on undifferentiated heavy lifting; analyze and attribute expenditure.

- **AWS examples:** S3 Intelligent-Tiering for automatic storage class transitions, Savings Plans and Spot for compute commitment and spare capacity, cost allocation tags for attribution, AWS Budgets for control.

### 6.5.6 Sustainability

- **Definition:** minimizing the environmental impact of running cloud workloads.

- **Design principles:** understand your impact; establish sustainability goals; maximize utilization; anticipate and adopt more efficient hardware and software offerings; use managed services; reduce the downstream impact of your workloads.

- **AWS examples:** AWS Graviton-based instances for better performance per watt, right-sizing and scheduling to raise utilization, storage lifecycle policies to avoid retaining data nobody reads, the AWS Customer Carbon Footprint Tool for measurement.

**A worked illustration of all six**

A single small architecture can be read through every pillar at once:

- Deploy EC2 instances across two Availability Zones behind a load balancer, for reliability.

- Enable AWS CloudTrail and enforce least privilege IAM roles, for security.

- Define the whole environment in CloudFormation, for operational excellence.

- Use Auto Scaling so capacity tracks demand, for performance efficiency.

- Store objects in S3 Intelligent-Tiering, for cost optimization.

- Raise average utilization through scaling and scheduling, and choose Graviton instances, for sustainability.

Notice that several of these decisions serve more than one pillar. Auto Scaling improves reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization, and sustainability at once. Those are the changes worth prioritizing.

## 6.6 General Design Principles

These apply across all six pillars.

- **Stop guessing your capacity needs.** Provision to actual demand and scale automatically, rather than sizing for a forecast.

- **Test systems at production scale.** In the cloud a full-scale test environment can be created for the duration of the test and destroyed afterwards, so testing at a fraction of scale is a choice rather than a constraint.

- **Automate to make architectural experimentation easier.** If creating an environment is cheap and repeatable, more alternatives get tried and better designs get chosen.

- **Allow for evolutionary architectures.** Design so the architecture can change, because the requirements will. A design that can only be replaced rather than modified will be neither.

- **Drive architectures using data.** Base decisions on measurements from the workload rather than on assumption or habit.

- **Improve through game days.** Rehearse failure and load events deliberately, so that recovery procedures are known to work rather than assumed to.

The connecting idea is that these principles are only affordable in cloud. Testing at production scale, rehearsing disaster recovery, and running experiments all require the ability to create and destroy infrastructure cheaply, which is what Chapter 1 called treating infrastructure as software.

## 6.7 The AWS Well-Architected Tool

The AWS Well-Architected Tool is a free service in the AWS Management Console that turns the framework into a structured review.

**How a review works**

1. Define the workload, including its Regions, its business owner, and its environment.

2. Select any applicable lenses in addition to the base framework.

3. Answer the questions for each pillar. Questions are answered against the workload as it actually is, not as intended.

4. Review the generated report, which lists identified risks by severity and links to relevant AWS documentation and guidance.

5. Record an improvement plan, then re-run the review later to measure progress. Milestones capture the state at a point in time so that change is visible.

---
**Practical notes**

- Answer honestly. A review completed to produce a clean report is worth nothing, and the risk items are the entire output.

- High risk items are not automatically things to fix. Some are accepted trade offs, and the tool provides somewhere to record that decision along with its reasoning.

- Reviews are most valuable before a major launch, after a significant incident, and on a regular cycle for workloads that matter.
