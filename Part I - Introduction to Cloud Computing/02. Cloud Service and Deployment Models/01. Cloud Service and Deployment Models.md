# Chapter 2: Cloud Service and Deployment Models

---
Two separate questions get confused constantly, so it is worth separating them before anything else.

- **Service model** answers "how much of the stack does the provider run?" The answers are IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS.

- **Deployment model** answers "who owns and who can use the environment?" The answers are public, private, hybrid, and community.

>They are independent. A private cloud can offer IaaS. A public cloud can offer SaaS. Sections 2.1 to 2.4 cover the first question, and sections 2.5 to 2.9 cover the second.

---
## 2.1 IaaS: Infrastructure as a Service

- **What the provider supplies:** compute, storage, and networking as virtualized resources, plus the physical facilities and hypervisor beneath them.

- **What you manage:** the operating system, patching, runtime, middleware, application code, and data.

- **Control level:** highest of the three models, and correspondingly the most work.

- **AWS examples:** Amazon EC2, Amazon EBS, Amazon VPC.

- **Other providers:** Azure Virtual Machines, Google Compute Engine.

**When it fits**

- The application needs a specific operating system, kernel version, or agent that a managed platform will not permit.

- You are migrating an existing server workload and want to change as little as possible during the move.

- Licensing or compliance requires control over the operating system layer.

**Example.** A company runs a legacy application that requires a particular Linux distribution and a vendor agent installed at the OS level. It launches EC2 instances from a custom AMI, installs the agent, attaches EBS volumes for data, and keeps its existing patching process. AWS runs the hardware and hypervisor. Everything above that stays with the company.

---
## 2.2 PaaS: Platform as a Service

- **What the provider supplies:** everything in IaaS, plus the operating system, runtime, and the deployment and scaling machinery.

- **What you manage:** your application code and your data.

- **Control level:** moderate. You give up OS-level access and gain back the time spent maintaining it.

- **AWS examples:** AWS Elastic Beanstalk, AWS App Runner, Amazon RDS for the database layer.

- **Other providers:** Heroku, Google App Engine, Azure App Service.

**When it fits**

- A development team wants to deploy code without owning servers.

- The workload is a standard web application or API in a supported language.

- Speed of delivery matters more than fine-grained infrastructure control.


**Example.** A team packages a Python web application and uploads it to Elastic Beanstalk. Beanstalk provisions the EC2 instances, load balancer, and Auto Scaling group, installs the runtime, deploys the code, and handles subsequent releases. The team never logs into a server, though it could if it needed to.

---
## 2.3 SaaS: Software as a Service

- **What the provider supplies:** the entire stack, including the application itself.

- **What you manage:** your data inside the application, your users, and your configuration.

- **Control level:** lowest, and the operational burden is close to zero.

- **AWS examples:** Amazon WorkSpaces, Amazon Connect, AWS Wickr.

- **Other providers:** Google Workspace, Salesforce, Slack, Microsoft 365.

**When it fits**

- The capability is not a competitive differentiator, so building it would be wasted effort.

- Users need access from a browser with no client installation or infrastructure behind it.

- The vendor should be responsible for updates, availability, and security patching of the application.

**Example.** A company adopts a hosted productivity suite. Staff sign in through a browser, the vendor ships updates and security patches without a maintenance window, and the company's only administrative work is managing users, licenses, and sharing policies.

---
## 2.4 Comparing the Three Models

The clearest way to see the difference is to lay out the stack and mark who is responsible for each layer.

| Layer | On-premises | IaaS | PaaS | SaaS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Applications | You | You | You | Provider |
| Data | You | You | You | You |
| Runtime | You | You | Provider | Provider |
| Middleware | You | You | Provider | Provider |
| Operating system | You | You | Provider | Provider |
| Virtualization | You | Provider | Provider | Provider |
| Servers | You | Provider | Provider | Provider |
| Storage | You | Provider | Provider | Provider |
| Networking | You | Provider | Provider | Provider |


- **Data is always yours.** In every model, including SaaS, responsibility for the data itself does not transfer. This is the seed of the shared responsibility model in Chapter 8.

- **Real services sit between the labels.** Amazon RDS is more than IaaS, because AWS patches the database engine, but less than PaaS, because you still choose an instance class and manage the schema. AWS Lambda does not fit any of the three cleanly, which is why serverless is usually treated as its own category.

- **AWS rarely uses these labels itself.** They come from the wider industry and remain useful for exams and for conversations with vendors, not for looking up a service in AWS documentation.

---
## 2.5 Public Cloud

An environment owned and operated by a provider and made available to any customer over the internet.

- **Providers:** AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle Cloud, IBM Cloud.

- **Advantages:** no infrastructure investment, capacity available immediately, global footprint, consumption-based pricing, and access to the provider's full service catalog.

- **Disadvantages:** physical infrastructure is shared with other tenants, data residency needs deliberate design, and customization stops at what the provider exposes.

- **Example:** provision an S3 bucket and an EC2 instance, control access with IAM roles, and serve the workload to users anywhere through public endpoints.


> Note: On shared infrastructure multi-tenancy is a common objection and usually a misplaced one. Tenants are isolated by the hypervisor and by the network, workloads can be placed on dedicated hosts when a license or regulation demands it, and the provider's security investment normally exceeds what a single organization can fund. Where the concern is genuine is data residency and legal jurisdiction, which are answered by Region selection rather than by rejecting public cloud.

---
## 2.6 Private Cloud

Cloud infrastructure dedicated to a single organization, run either in its own data center or by a third party on its behalf.

- **Examples:** VMware Cloud Foundation, OpenStack, Azure Stack.

- **Advantages:** full control over hardware and configuration, easier answers to strict regulatory and data residency requirements, and predictable performance without noisy neighbors.

- **Disadvantages:** capital cost returns, capacity is finite and must still be planned, specialist staff are required, and the service catalog is whatever you build yourself.

- **Example:** deploy OpenStack on dedicated hardware, create virtual networks and storage pools, and retain complete control of security policy and compliance evidence.


>A private cloud gives you cloud like provisioning without cloud economics. You keep the capacity guessing problem from Chapter 1, and you do not get the massive economies of scale. It is the right answer when a specific legal, latency, or sovereignty constraint makes it the right answer, and an expensive one otherwise.

---
## 2.7 Hybrid Cloud

Public and private environments connected so that data and applications can move or interoperate between them.

- **Tools:** AWS Direct Connect and AWS Site-to-Site VPN for connectivity, AWS Outposts to run AWS infrastructure on-premises, AWS Storage Gateway and AWS DataSync for storage integration, AWS Directory Service for shared identity.

- **Advantages:** sensitive or latency-bound workloads stay on-premises while everything else uses public cloud capacity, existing hardware investment continues to earn out, and migration can happen in stages rather than as one event.

- **Disadvantages:** two environments to secure, monitor, and staff, network design becomes load-bearing, and identity and access must be unified or it will drift.

- **Example:** connect an on-premises data center to a VPC over Direct Connect, replicate a database into AWS with AWS DMS, and use one identity source across both sides.

>Hybrid is the normal state for most large organizations during migration, and for many of them permanently. Chapter 22 covers the connectivity options in detail, and Chapter 29 covers migration strategy.

---
## 2.8 Community Cloud

Infrastructure shared by several organizations with common requirements, typically regulatory, security, or mission-related.

- **Examples:** research consortia sharing computing and data platforms, and government community environments built to a shared compliance standard.

- **Advantages:** cost and governance are shared across members, the environment is built for the specific compliance regime the members face, and collaboration on shared datasets is straightforward.

- **Disadvantages:** more expensive per unit than public cloud, governance requires agreement between independent organizations, membership is closed, and scale is limited compared with a major provider.

- **Example:** several research institutions join a shared biomedical research platform, pool compute and storage, and operate under a mutual agreement covering privacy, compliance, and data sharing.

>Community cloud appears least often in practice and least often on exams. Recognize the definition and move on.

## 2.9 Choosing a Deployment Model

| Requirement | Points toward |
| --- | --- |
| No capital budget, needs capacity now | Public |
| Regulation requires data in a named country | Public, with Region selection, or private if no compliant Region exists |
| Hardware or license tied to physical control | Private |
| Sub-millisecond latency to on-premises equipment | Hybrid, or AWS Outposts |
| Existing data center with years left on the lease | Hybrid |
| Migrating a large estate over several years | Hybrid during transition |
| Several organizations sharing one compliance regime and one dataset | Community |
| Anything else | Public |


**A short decision sequence that works in practice:**

1. Identify any hard constraint: a law, a contract, a latency figure, or a license tied to physical hardware.

2. If no hard constraint exists, choose public cloud. The economics in Chapter 3 make this the default.

3. If a constraint exists, check whether public cloud already answers it. Region selection covers most data residency rules, and dedicated hosts cover most licensing rules.

4. Only when the constraint survives step 3 should the design move to hybrid or private, and it should move only the affected component, not the whole estate.


> AWS training material frequently frames this as three choices, cloud, hybrid, and on-premises, rather than the four-model taxonomy above. The two framings do not conflict. Know both, since exam questions may use either.
